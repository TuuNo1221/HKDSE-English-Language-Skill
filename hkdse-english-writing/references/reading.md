# Paper 1 reading

For 2027, Paper 1 lasts 90 minutes. Complete Part A, then either B1 (easier, with a maximum of Level 4 on the paper) or B2 (full range available). Use the paper's own instructions for the year, section, and answer format. [HKEAA 2027 framework](https://www.hkeaa.edu.hk/DocLibrary/HKDSE/Subject_Information/eng_lang/2027hkdse-e-elang.pdf)

## Read from passage to sentence to word

1. Check the title, source, genre, paragraph breaks, and any images or captions. Skim for the situation, the writer's purpose, and how the ideas change across paragraphs. Give each important paragraph a short label such as opening problem, example, contrasting view, or recommendation.
2. Classify each question by what it asks the answer to do. Note the line or paragraph range, required length, and whether to quote, paraphrase, choose an option, or complete a gap.
3. Locate the answer-bearing sentence and read around it. Match meaning across paraphrases. Check qualifiers such as *some*, *only*, *may*, and *always*, plus speaker, time, scope, and cause.
4. Give the shortest complete answer in the requested form. In a worked solution, pair it with the relevant line or short phrase and explain the reasoning separately. Use line numbers only when the supplied passage has them. Flag missing text or ambiguous items.

## Question types

| Type | Method |
| --- | --- |
| True / False / Not Given | True when the passage supports the whole statement. False when it contradicts a necessary part. Not Given when a necessary part is neither established nor contradicted. Check subject, quantity, time, and certainty. |
| Sequence | Recover event order from tense, time markers, and dependencies. A flashback can change the order in which events are mentioned. |
| Proofreading and summary cloze | Read the completed sentence for meaning and grammar. Follow the instructions on copying, changes to word form, and answer length. Check agreement and verb tense after filling the gap. |
| Find a word or phrase | Search only the requested range. Match meaning and grammatical role, then test the candidate in context. Copy the exact form when asked. |
| Meaning in context | Infer the sense from the immediate situation and adjacent sentences. A “why” follow-up may require both the contextual meaning and the reason it applies. A dictionary gloss on its own may miss the point. |
| Reference | Check nearby candidate nouns or ideas for number, grammar, and meaning. Substitute the full candidate into the sentence. A phrase may refer to an entire preceding idea. |
| Literal question, inference, or speaker attribution | Identify the requested person, reason, method, or consequence. For inference, connect specific clues and distinguish a speaker's opinion from the writer's view. |
| Main idea, paragraph purpose, or title | Summarise the passage or paragraph and identify its function. Test options for coverage and scope. An option can be true of a detail and still be too narrow. |
| Tone, attitude, or figurative language | Use specific wording and context. For a comparison, name both sides and their shared quality. For irony, explain the gap between what is said or expected and what the passage shows. Explain the effect when asked. |
| Visuals or likely source | Match captions, labels, and details to the text. Infer likely publication or audience from the genre, register, and layout where the question asks for it. |

## Worked examples

Original practice extract. “The library extended its hours on Fridays. The librarian called it a lifeline for students who have nowhere quiet to work. She said the timetable might change after exams.”

- **“The library now closes at 9 pm on Fridays.”** Not Given. The passage says it stays open longer and gives no closing time.
- **“The new timetable is guaranteed to continue after exams.”** False. The librarian allows for a change.
- **“Why does the librarian call it a lifeline?”** The extended hours give students without a quiet place to work somewhere to study for longer. In context, *lifeline* describes the practical support the change provides.

For practice, create an original passage and questions with enough textual evidence to distinguish each answer from plausible distractors. Keep the key separate, or reveal answers one at a time during a drill.

## Work through a question set

- Locate evidence before selecting from closely worded options. For each rejected option, identify the changed detail, reversed relationship, unsupported inference, or overly broad claim.
- For source-word retrieval, record the search range, word class, and whether the question asks for an equivalent or an opposite. For interpretation, temporarily bracket the expression mentally, work out the surrounding situation, then test the inferred meaning in the complete sentence. A why-question can require a distinct causal step after the interpretation.
- For proofreading, compare meaningful chunks with the passage. Identify the smallest offending unit and obey the exact replacement and marking instructions. For cloze, predict word class, match the source idea, then check the whole completed clause.
- Track references and voices on a separate pass in dialogue or opinion pieces. For attribution, test the proposed speaker against their earlier remarks, access to information, and attitude. For a pronoun, try a noun, clause, or previously mentioned situation.
- For title and purpose questions, write a short answer before checking the options. Identify whether a paragraph introduces, illustrates, qualifies, contrasts, or concludes. For tone, name the attitude and what it is directed at. A striking word is a clue to check against the surrounding sentences.
- For open responses, use the question word to decide what to supply. Give a cause for *why*, a method or mechanism for *how*, and paired features for a comparison. When asked to explain a metaphor, state what quality transfers from the image to the subject. When asked about irony, state the expectation and what actually happens.
