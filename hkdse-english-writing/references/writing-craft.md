# Writing craft and literary devices

## Narrative design

- Start with the assigned characters, event, or ending if provided. Give the main character a goal, a credible obstacle, and a choice. Let the choice affect the outcome. Escalate through events that make sense together and give the conclusion enough space to pay off the central conflict.
- The notes include conflicts with self, another person, society, nature, the supernatural, technology, and circumstances. Plot shapes include betrayal, a quest, a journey with a return, changes of fortune, and relationships that alter over time. Treat these as ways to organise a specific story.
- Open with action, an unusual observation, a clue, dialogue, setting, or a reflective line. If using a flashback, establish the current moment and make the time shift readable. Use sentence length, paragraph breaks, precise verbs, sound, and selective sensory detail to manage pace.
- Reveal character through word choice, physical action, private thoughts available to the narrator, other characters' reactions, and direct description when efficient. Establish first person, third-person limited, or omniscient access. Keep the viewpoint consistent unless a shift is deliberate and clear.
- An ending can resolve the choice, return to an opening image, change the character's perspective, reveal an earlier clue, or sustain deliberate uncertainty. Possible reversals include a false clue, a hidden identity, an earlier detail acquiring a new meaning, changed fortune, a repeated scene, or an unreliable narrator. Plant sufficient clues for a reveal and avoid an unexplained late rescue.
- Use mood for the effect on the reader and tone for the writer's or narrator's attitude. Show their basis in detail, syntax, diction, or point of view. Dialogue should reveal something, advance action, or change the relationship.

## Description, titles, and persuasive devices

| Technique | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Sensory detail, motion, and precise nouns | Select details that establish time, location, emotion, or viewpoint. A strong verb can remove the need for several modifiers. |
| Simile, metaphor, personification | Use a shared quality the reader can recognise. In analysis, identify what is compared and explain the effect in context. |
| Sound and rhythm | Use repetition, alliteration, rhyme, parallelism, lists, sentence length, and pauses selectively. Notice the meaning the pattern emphasises. |
| Irony, contrast, and hyperbole | Give the reader enough context to recognise the gap between literal language, expectations, and events. Use emphasis without inventing factual support. |
| Direct address, rhetoric, and commands | Match questions, pronouns, imperatives, anecdotes, emotional images, humour, or calls to action to the relationship with readers. Treat an attributed quotation or statistic as a factual claim to verify. |
| Headline construction | Try a question, a short noun phrase, an adjective-noun phrase, a verb phrase, an alliterative phrase, a paired title and subtitle, or a character-centred relative clause. Choose the one that points to the actual angle. |

The notes' sentence-opening exercises vary subject-first openings with time and place phrases, participle phrases, clauses, dialogue, appositives, and reordered emphasis. Give each variation a reason, such as pace, clarity, viewpoint, or focus. Check subjects after introductory modifiers and punctuate interruptions and dialogue consistently.

**Original drill.** Rewrite “She was nervous when the doors opened” twice. First show the reaction through action. Then write the moment as a line of dialogue with an action beat. Compare how each version controls what the reader learns.
