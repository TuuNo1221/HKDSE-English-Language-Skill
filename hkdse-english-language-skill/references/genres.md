# Genre and paragraph choices

The prompt determines the required format. Use this guide as a set of drafting choices.

| Text type | Opening and layout | Development and ending |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Informal advice letter or email | Greet the recipient by first name. Acknowledge their specific situation and show empathy. | Suggest one workable action per paragraph, with a reason or a first step. Close with encouragement or an invitation to follow up. |
| Formal email or letter | Use the prompt's recipient, subject line if appropriate, salutation, and role. State the purpose early. | Group information by request or issue. A complaint describes the problem and seeks a specific remedy. A reply addresses each issue and explains the available action. With a conventional British formal letter, use “Yours faithfully” for an unnamed recipient and “Yours sincerely” for a named one. |
| Letter to the editor | Address the editor. Introduce the public issue and identify a source article if the prompt supplies it. | Explain how the issue affects readers or the community. End with a proportionate recommendation or call to action. |
| Feature article or blog post | Use a fitting headline or title. Open with a concrete situation or question that matters to the intended reader. | Develop the subject with examples and a consistent voice. Close with an insight, invitation, or next step that serves the purpose. |
| Opinion, argumentative, or discursive essay | Establish the issue and scope. State a stance early if the task asks for one. | An argument develops reasons and engages with a relevant objection. A discussion weighs the requested perspectives against comparable criteria. Conclude in line with the prompt. |
| Speech or debate speech | Greet the actual audience. Introduce your role as needed and state the purpose. In a debate, establish your side and clarify the motion where relevant. | Use audible signposting and direct address. For rebuttal, identify a plausible objection, explain its limitation, then support your side. End with a takeaway or a clear reaffirmation of your stance. |
| Report or proposal | State the purpose. Use section headings if they help. A survey report can describe the method when it is supplied or requested. | A report presents findings and any requested recommendations. A proposal explains the need, action, implementation, and benefits, then asks for the relevant decision. |
| Witness report or factual recount | Establish who, when and where with relevant details. | Give events in an intelligible order and distinguish observations from inferences. End with the final relevant event or requested outcome. |
| Review | Identify the work, event, or product and give readers enough context to follow the evaluation. | Evaluate specific features with examples and recommend it to a suitable audience if called for. Keep plot summary brief. |
| Story or diary | A story can open with a character in a concrete scene. A diary uses a personal voice and reflects on an experience. | Build a story through conflict, choices, and consequences. A diary can end with a changed feeling, question, or intention. |
| Notice, circular, leaflet, or newsletter | Make the subject and intended readers apparent. Use headings and short blocks where helpful. | Make actions, practical details, and contacts easy to find when the prompt provides them. |
| News report or editorial | Use a clear headline. Lead a news report with the event; introduce the issue and position in an editorial. | Attribute claims and use an informative tone in news. Develop the editorial's position with reasons and a suitable recommendation. |

## Developing a point

Use **point → elaboration → evidence or example → link** as a planning check. Ask what happens, to whom, how it happens, and why it matters. Choose the questions that add substance. The link can be implicit if the consequence already answers the question.

**Original example, school magazine argument.** A homework club would make after-school help easier to access. Students who share devices at home could use the library computers and ask a teacher about a difficult assignment before leaving. Imagine a student who notices a mistake in her spreadsheet during the session and has time to fix it before the deadline. Regular access to that kind of help would let students act on problems while there is still time.

For a rebuttal, acknowledge the concrete objection, identify a condition it overlooks, and explain what your response would change. A concern about teacher workload could lead to a weekly pilot with one staff supervisor and a rota of trained student volunteers, provided the prompt allows those details.

Use rhetorical questions, parallel lists, a brief anecdote, or direct address only when they clarify an idea or draw the intended reader into it. Choose a specific scenario over a famous quotation with uncertain wording or attribution.

## Advice in context

| Purpose | Pattern | Original example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer a low-pressure option | could + base verb | You could ask a classmate to rehearse with you. |
| Show how to start | try + -ing | Try recording your opening once and listening for where you rush. |
| Recommend a plan | suggest + -ing / suggest that + subject + base verb | I suggest that you practise the first minute separately. |
| Give personal advice | If I were you, I'd + base verb | If I were you, I'd focus on one improvement at a time. |
| Explain the payoff | so that / by + -ing | Record yourself so that you can spot where you need a pause. |

Use stronger modals for real obligations or urgent concerns. For sensitive situations, acknowledge what the reader is dealing with before offering advice.

## Planning and development from the notes

The notes use three prompt features to decide what to write. The **situation** sets vocabulary and register, the **text type** sets structure and conventions, and the **sub-questions** set content coverage. Make a coverage map with one row per required point. For Part B prompt selection, sketch the strongest evidence and genre fit for each plausible choice before committing.

**Openings.** Choose only the background the reader needs. Options include setting, immediate situation, a trend, a representative case, a definition, or a broader phenomenon. After that, establish the purpose, stance, or central question. In a solicited reply, acknowledge the original request before explaining what this reply will do.

**Body.** The notes expand PEEL as Point, Elaboration, Evidence, Link. Ask who, what, when, where, why, and how selectively. A body paragraph can explain a claim through a cause, benefit, drawback, impact, or method. A story develops through actions and consequences. A practical report groups factual details under meaningful sections.

**Evidence and persuasion.** MIINAH groups made-up data, idioms, icons, news, authority, and history. The data category is suitable only for explicitly invented scenarios where the prompt permits it. Label hypothetical data as such, and give the real source for a real figure or quotation. An idiom can make a point memorable. The evidence is the event, mechanism, or observation that supports it. For real articles, public figures, talks, organisations, or events, keep the claim narrow and verifiable. Personal experience can illustrate an individual effect when the role and genre support it. It has limited weight as evidence for a general claim.

**Rebuttal.** The notes use OTPEEL for an opposition claim, a pivot or response, and a developed point. State the objection in a form its supporter would accept. Acknowledge its strongest element, then explain which assumption fails or how the proposal can answer the concern. Give a concrete example and reconnect to the motion. Use a rebuttal where it advances the argument, including after establishing the main position.

**Endings.** Choose a next step, recommendation, prediction, implication, hope, or reflection that follows from the text. An editor letter can make an actionable request and explain the likely consequence of delay. Advice can close with encouragement and a follow-up invitation. A witness report can end at the final observation.

## Genre decisions that commonly affect draft quality

- **Advice and suggestions.** Acknowledge the issue without assuming the friend's exact feelings. Give an action, an achievable first step, and a reason it could help. Match force to urgency. Use a first-name sign-off or prompt-specified identity.
- **Complaint and adjustment.** A complaint establishes the transaction or incident, then gives factual problems, effects, and the requested remedy. A reply acknowledges the inconvenience, explains each relevant issue, and states what the organisation can do and when.
- **Proposal and report.** A proposal describes need, actions, resources or responsibilities, feasibility, and the decision requested. A survey report separates method, findings, interpretation, and recommendations where relevant. Preserve denominators and uncertainty where the data supplies them.
- **Discursive and argumentative writing.** A discursive piece gives each relevant view a fair account and compares it on consistent grounds. An opinion piece develops a position with reasons. For problem and solution tasks, connect each action to a cause or impact and identify who could implement it.
- **Headlines and bylines.** Compose the title after identifying the angle. A short noun phrase, question, verb phrase, or paired title and subtitle can work. Use a byline when the task and genre call for one. A report title prioritises retrieval, while a feature title can invite interest.
- **Narrative and descriptive writing.** Establish setting and viewpoint, introduce a conflict, raise the stakes, and resolve or deliberately leave the central tension open. Characterise through actions, dialogue, and what other people notice. Use sensory details and precise verbs to direct attention. Choose a chronological opening, action in progress, dialogue, a description, or a signposted flashback. Keep tense and viewpoint consistent. Distinguish first person, third-person limited, and an omniscient narrator.
- **News, review, and editorial.** News leads with the most important verified event and attributes information. Reviews give only enough synopsis to support evaluation, then judge specific features and identify a suitable audience. Editorials establish the publication's stance and support a recommendation.
- **Public and promotional texts.** Use concrete benefits, readable layout, and a clear action. A well-placed question, contrast, list of three, anecdote, imperative, alliteration, or repetition can make an argument easier to follow. Match rhetorical strength to genre and audience.
