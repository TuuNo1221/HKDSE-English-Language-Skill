# Paper 3 integrated writing

Use this workflow for the writing tasks supported by a prompt, data file, and listening input. Consult the year-specific instructions for timing and task structure. [HKEAA 2027 framework](https://www.hkeaa.edu.hk/DocLibrary/HKDSE/Subject_Information/eng_lang/2027hkdse-e-elang.pdf)

1. List each deliverable and its writer, reader, purpose, format, and required points. Break compound requests into components. Retain any supplied starter text, required headings, or recipient details.
2. For each point, record the source location (page, message, table, or recording timestamp) and the usable fact. Scan across sources before drafting. The same document may serve several tasks. Irrelevant material can be left out.
3. Confirm status and timing. A tentative plan, confirmed decision, cancellation, and invitation to suggest options require different language. If dates or numbers clash, check whether a later update clearly supersedes the earlier one. If the conflict remains, flag it.
4. Combine relevant facts in the assigned voice. Paraphrase without changing meaning, preserve exact names and figures where required, and convert source language into the deliverable's register. Expand shorthand only when its meaning is clear. A note from a colleague may become a formal sentence in an external email.
5. Check every usable requirement against the finished text and verify names, dates, figures, units, links, and conditions. Keep source labels in the planning notes or feedback unless the deliverable itself calls for attribution.
6. If a source is missing, ask for it when it is necessary for a complete answer. With a partial set, draft the supported portions and list unresolved facts outside the answer. Use clearly marked placeholders only if the user asks for a template or explicitly accepts them.

**Original example.** A task asks the secretary to email teachers about the new room and arrival time. The old timetable lists Room 204 at 3.45 pm, a later caretaker's message confirms the move to Room 108, and the briefing asks teachers to arrive ten minutes early. The email should tell teachers to come to Room 108 by 3.35 pm, provided the revised message changes only the room. If the briefing's time reference is missing, request it before asserting an arrival time.

## Part A listening practice

1. Preview the task and predict what each gap or question needs: a name, number, time, reason, action, or short phrase. Notice headings, plurals, units, and words already printed around the gap.
2. Capture meaningful details with brief symbols or abbreviations. Write unfamiliar names and topic terms as fully as possible. Distinguish speakers, proposals, confirmation, and corrections. If an answer is missed, keep listening for the next one.
3. Convert notes to the requested answer form. Check whether a short phrase or a complete answer is needed. Proofread articles, agreement, tense, plural and singular forms, parallel structure, capitalisation, spelling, and punctuation. Keep answers faithful to the recording.
4. For study drills, split feedback into missed signal, wrong interpretation, inaccurate transcription, and output-form errors. With a transcript alone, practise locating and interpreting details. Use supplied audio to assess listening performance.

## Part B classroom planning and PEARLS formats

The notes split preparation into planning the deliverables, taking listening notes, locating data-file points, and composing. Use a simple matrix with a row for each prompt requirement and columns for source, detail, destination, and status. Preview the deliverables before the relevant recording when the paper permits. Match notes and the data file across all tasks, then compose each answer in the assigned voice. Use the year-specific paper cues and timing. Treat the notes' suggested minute allocations and target scores as practice heuristics.

| PEARLS format | Useful layout and content choices |
| --- | --- |
| Proposal | Use a heading and byline if appropriate. Introduce background, problem, and purpose. Group actions by implementation topic. End with the decision requested. |
| Email | Use recipient, subject, greeting, and supplied sender details as needed. A reply acknowledges the earlier message. State the purpose, group the requested points, and close with a next action or contact. |
| Article | Use a caption or headline and any requested byline. Give the audience the relevant background and purpose, develop the points in readable order, and close with the intended takeaway. |
| Report | Use a heading and author where relevant. State the purpose, organise findings under headings that match the task, and give any requested recommendation. Preserve facts and qualifications. |
| Letter and letter to the editor | Use the prompt's salutation, date and sign-off conventions. An editor letter gives the referenced source when supplied, the issue, and the writer's position. A reply addresses the incoming points, then states the action. |
| Speech | Address the audience. Establish role and topic, acknowledge the occasion if relevant, and signpost content for listeners. Summarise or give a next step, then thank the audience if appropriate. |

Use ordinary paragraphs with transitions when the deliverable benefits from continuous prose. Use short, specific subheadings when the reader needs to retrieve items quickly. Distinguish topic transitions such as *regarding* from attribution such as *according to*. Use *in accordance with* for agreement with a rule or instruction.

**Practice check.** The initial notice lists 40 seats. The recording says 10 more seats were approved and also specifies that the workshop is adults only. A registration announcement needs the revised capacity of 50 and the eligibility restriction. The internal approval trail belongs in the source map unless the audience needs it.
